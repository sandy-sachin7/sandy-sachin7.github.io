When I first picked up *Sapiens*, I thought I was signing up for a history lesson. What I got instead was a high-speed drone flight over 70,000 years of humanity — starting from a tiny East African primate to the Bangalore techie tapping UPI at 2x speed.

It didn't just explain how humans got here. It explained **why we behave the way we do** — in offices, in markets, in relationships, and even in the gym. So here's my own retelling of *Sapiens*, through my eyes, my background, and my life in 2025.

## 1. The Superpower of Storytelling — The Cognitive Revolution

Yuval Noah Harari says our greatest weapon wasn't claws or speed — it was **imagination**. Around 70,000 years ago, humans started creating *shared fictions*: myths, religions, nations, companies.

It hit me that Bangalore itself runs on this — not just on code, but on **shared beliefs**:

- We believe "money" exists, though we can't touch most of it (hello, UPI and CoinDCX crypto wallets).
- We believe a company exists because a piece of paper says so — and because we all agree it does.
- We follow traffic lights not because they're physically powerful, but because we *agree* to follow them… most of the time.

In the gym, when I track progress, I'm following my own "imagined order" — a belief that numbers on a spreadsheet represent a stronger, better me. Without this ability to create and believe shared fictions, there's no SIP portfolio, no nation, no tech boom.

## 2. When Progress Becomes a Trap — The Agricultural Revolution

Harari calls farming "history's biggest fraud." Sure, it let us produce more food, but it chained us to fields, increased disease, and reduced leisure time. Farmers didn't work for *freedom* — they worked to *not starve*.

It reminded me of corporate life: promotions, raises, better titles — but also more deadlines, late-night calls, and Slack pings. Just like wheat domesticated us (not the other way around), sometimes work *owns us*.

It's why I guard my time now — fitting in gym, self-learning, relationships, and portfolio reviews like they're my crops. Except, I make sure these "crops" feed me, not trap me.

## 3. The Great Human Merge — Trade, Money, Empires, Religion

Over thousands of years, humans built bigger communities using three main tools:

- **Money** — the universal language
- **Empires** — political systems that created stability (and sometimes oppression)
- **Religion** — moral frameworks to trust strangers

For me, this clicked with investing. My mutual funds work because millions of people believe in the "order" of markets. Crypto works because enough of us believe in decentralization. Without that shared trust, my SIP plan is just numbers in an app.

Bangalore is basically a modern empire of coders — we may come from different states, speak different languages, but we share the "religion" of deadlines, Jira tickets, and ESOP/RSUs dreams.

## 4. The Scientific Mindset — The Revolution That Changed Everything

Until about 500 years ago, humans thought they *already knew* everything. The Scientific Revolution happened when we admitted, "We don't know — but we can find out."

That humility drives my own learning — whether it's improving a workout plan, debugging code, or rebalancing my portfolio. If you're not updating your mental software, you're living in a pre-science world.

## 5. Capitalism's Rise — Credit, Growth, and Risk

Capitalism was born from the idea that tomorrow can be better than today — so you can invest money *now* for a bigger pie later. It's literally my investment thesis: ₹20k SIP, 10% annual step-up, with some crypto for asymmetric upside.

But Harari also warns: capitalism is great at growth, bad at deciding **what to grow**. That's why I think about not just "how much wealth" but "why wealth." Without that, you're just farming more wheat without asking if you even like bread.

## 6. The Happiness Paradox

Here's the gut punch: more money, food, and tech hasn't made us much happier than hunter-gatherers. Our wants just keep inflating.

I've felt that — new job, joining bonus, better salary… and within weeks, my brain wants *the next thing*. Harari suggests happiness is more about community, meaning, and personal challenge. For me, that's:

- Gym PRs over gadgets
- Strong SIP over impulse buys
- Deep conversations over scrolling

Happiness is less "what I have" and more "how I frame what I have."

## 7. Where We're Heading — The Next Chapter of Sapiens

We might be the last generation of *Homo sapiens* as we know it. AI, bioengineering, genetic editing — we're on the verge of designing life itself. Reading this made me look at my own industry (tech + healthcare) differently. We're already building systems that blur human-machine lines. The next "species" might not be born in nature — it might be *uploaded*.

It's thrilling… and a bit terrifying.

## Closing Reflection — The Zoomed-Out Lens

*Sapiens* didn't just give me history; it gave me perspective. When I zoom out far enough, my daily worries look tiny — one Jira ticket in the massive backlog of human history. But it also reminded me that **small actions ripple forward** — a hunter's choice to follow a new trail, a farmer's choice to plant wheat, a coder's choice to ship a feature.

My takeaway? Play the long game. Build systems (in health, relationships, wealth, and work) that would make sense even if I were telling the story 1,000 years from now.

Here's my Tip ;)

> If you read *Sapiens*, don't just look for "facts." Look for **lenses** — because once you see through them, you can't unsee the world the same way.
